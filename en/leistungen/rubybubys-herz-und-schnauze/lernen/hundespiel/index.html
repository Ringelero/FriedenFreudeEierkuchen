Understanding & learning

Still playing, or is one dog being overwhelmed?

Notice the sequence, breaks and voluntary contact. Includes a real-video exercise.

Your dog runs, the other follows. Both are fast and noisy. Then yours disappears behind your legs. Was that still fun? The important question is whether both dogs can shape and end the interaction. Here you learn to watch the sequence and allow a break before either dog has to become more forceful.

Watch the other dog's response

Look at each dog in turn. Who initiates contact? What does the other do in response? And what happens when one slows down or moves away?

Signs supporting play include loose, bouncy movement, a relaxed face, interruptions and changing roles. A stronger dog may slow down or make themselves smaller. Consider these together; a single play bow does not decide the situation. The play check from Sprich Hund!.

Do not count wins. You do not need a record showing exactly equal time on top. A more useful question is: Does the active dog change their behaviour when the other wants less? Christina Young describes in the IAABC Foundation Journalhow ignored requests for a break can lead to increasingly one-sided interactions.

These differences change your decision

What you seeWhat to watch nextYour decision
One dog runs away.Do they return with a loose body? Or seek shelter and get pursued?Watch the return and the partner's response; allow shelter.
One lies on their back.Do they move loosely or remain tense while the other stays over them?Interrupt when they are tense and cannot move away.
Both growl while wrestling.Are movements loose? Are there pauses and responses to interruptions?Sound alone does not decide. Assess the whole sequence.
One turns away or approaches their person.Does the other let them go?If pressure continues, help them create distance.

Running, rolling over and growling can mean different things in different contexts. This interpretation draws on Young's article and Dogs Trust's body-language guide. You may end an interaction when unsure.

A break gives both dogs a choice again

Agree with the other person beforehand if possible: “When one wants to leave, we both call our dogs away.” Choose somewhere with room for distance rather than a narrow passage.

Call your dog using a familiar cue while they can still respond. The other person also keeps their dog out of contact. Move apart so that neither immediately follows the other. Give a reward beside you, away from the other dog.

Now watch: does your dog want to sniff, move on or stay with you? Allow that choice. If both seek contact again comfortably, you can consider another interaction. Dogs Trust recommends interruptions when play is unclear. A dog rushing straight back does not, by itself, prove relaxed play. Check that both retain space when contact resumes. Dogs Trust: assessing play.

If your dog no longer responds to recall, try interrupting earlier next time. During an escalating conflict, do not reach between dogs or grab their collars. Repeated conflicts need an individual plan with professional support.

An example of making the decision

An invented everyday example, not an analysis of a real video:

Mila chases Oskar. Initially, Oskar runs in a curve and bounces back. Later, he stands beside his person. Mila approaches again; Oskar turns his head and moves behind his person.

The useful observation is that Oskar has left the interaction but is not getting a break. Both people call their dogs apart. Oskar starts sniffing and does not seek renewed contact. The meeting continues as a walk with space. Nobody needs to decide whether Mila was “bad” or Oskar “too sensitive”.

What helps at the next meeting

Note the specific change: “After several chase rounds, he stayed beside me; the other kept following.” That helps your next arrangement more than “He doesn't like dogs.” You can pause earlier or choose a quieter partner.
