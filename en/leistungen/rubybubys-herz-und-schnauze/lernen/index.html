Take one idea with you and try it in a familiar, quiet environment. Your dog is free to move away. A break matters just as much as a successful attempt.

01Start by observing.Body language & encounters+

Posture, gaze, ears and movement together tell us more than a single sign. Also observe what's happening around your dog.

Do they seem relaxed?

A loose posture, gentle movements and a relaxed facial expression can be signs of this.

Do they need more space?

Turning away, crouching, freezing or a tense posture can indicate discomfort. Lip licking or yawning may also be relevant in a particular context.

A small observation

What changes with more distance?

  1. Observe how your dog looks in a familiar, quiet situation.
  2. During an encounter, observe the whole body. Make room if they move away or become tense.
  3. See whether they relax again. Contact doesn't have to happen; you can keep walking.

Pause now: If your dog freezes, withdraws or is clearly tense, stop approaching and create space.

Foundations: RSPCA · Body language and Dogs Trust · Stress and distance (English).

02Something good together.Learning through reinforcement+

Rewards help your dog understand which behaviour is worthwhile. A suitable reward depends on what they enjoy at that moment, such as food, play or friendly attention.

A small beginning

Notice a voluntary glance.

  1. Choose a quiet environment with few distractions and have a suitable reward ready.
  2. If your dog briefly looks at you of their own accord, immediately acknowledge the glance warmly and give them the reward.
  3. Repeat just a few times. End the short session while it still feels easy for both of you.

If they don't look at you, make the situation easier or try later. Don't pull them towards you or require prolonged eye contact.

Pause now: If your dog moves away, becomes restless or turns away, ease the demand. Learning needs neither punishment nor leash jerks.

Foundations: RSPCA · Short learning sessions, Dogs Trust · Suitable rewards and AVSAB · Reward-based learning (English).

03Calm can grow too.Retreat & relaxed breaks+

A quiet place and a predictable routine can help with relaxation. Lots of exercise alone doesn't guarantee rest; an overtired dog can also struggle to settle.

An invitation to pause

Offer a comfortable place to rest.

  1. Put a familiar blanket or comfortable bed in a quiet place away from the bustle.
  2. Let your dog go there and get up again freely. Keep your voice and movements calm.
  3. If they lie down comfortably, let them rest undisturbed. Allow time to recover after exciting encounters.

Make things easier now: If they can't settle, reduce the noise and movement around them. Being held in place or having to stay on a blanket isn't relaxation.

Foundations: Dogs Trust · Restlessness and resting places and Dogs Trust · Allowing retreat (English).

When observation isn't enough.

If behaviour changes suddenly, severe restlessness persists or you suspect pain, have your dog examined by a vet. For recurring fear or conflicts, qualified behavioural support using reward-based methods can help.

RSPCA · Support with behavioural changes · Dogs Trust · Possible causes of restlessness

Our approach & sources

At Herz und Schnauze, we want to get to know animals, take their needs seriously and let relationships grow. Krishnamurti's thoughts on observation and relationships inspire this approach. Applying them to life with dogs is our own interpretation.

We have written the practical starting points ourselves, drawing on the animal behaviour foundations linked in each section. They are general suggestions, not an individual treatment plan. AVSAB recommends reward-based methods and rejects aversive methods.
