You do not need to learn everything at once. Choose one idea, observe your dog and take your time. Space and breaks are part of learning.

01How can my puppy explore the world?Puppies+

Settling in and discovering new things go together. Offer manageable experiences with recovery time in between. Your puppy's responses guide the pace; collecting as many encounters as possible is not the goal.

Three small steps

  1. Prepare an undisturbed retreat.
  2. Let them observe new things from a comfortable distance.
  3. Reward voluntary approaches and allow retreat.

When to take a step back: If they hide, freeze or move away, create space. Discuss safe outdoor contact with your vet.

02What is my dog trying to tell me?Body language+

Look at the whole situation: posture, facial expression and movement belong together. One signal cannot explain everything. Noticing changes early helps you respond by giving your dog more space when they need it.

Three small steps

  1. Notice relaxed or tense posture.
  2. Respect turning away and moving away.
  3. Note observations alongside the situation.

When to take a step back: Create space if they freeze or growl. Seek professional advice for recurring distress or sudden changes.

03What helps us learn together?Learning with rewards+

Learning is easier when the task is clear and the setting is calm. Notice voluntary behaviour that helps in everyday life and reward it straight away with something your dog enjoys.

Three small steps

  1. Choose a calm setting and a suitable reward.
  2. Immediately reward a voluntary glance towards you.
  3. Keep repetitions easy and brief, ending with a success.

When to take a step back: If your dog turns away or becomes restless, simplify the situation and take a break. Fear and pressure have no place in the exercise.

04How can dog encounters feel easier?Dog encounters+

An encounter does not need to become a greeting. Choose routes with space and ways out so your dog can notice others without feeling crowded. Passing calmly can already be a worthwhile shared success.

Three small steps

  1. Choose a distance where your dog can still respond.
  2. Reward calm observation and turning away.
  3. Take a wider route early when needed.

When to take a step back: Increase distance if they stare, freeze or lunge. Seek qualified behaviour support for repeated intense reactions.

05How can we walk together on a loose lead?Loose-lead walking+

Loose-lead walking grows from many small, understandable experiences. Start with few distractions and reward choosing to stay near you. Keep room for sniffing so a walk remains enjoyable rather than becoming a continuous obedience exercise.

Three small steps

  1. Choose comfortable, well-fitting equipment.
  2. Reward a few shared steps with a loose lead.
  3. Add distractions only when the current task feels easy.

When to take a step back: Simplify the setting if tension or difficulty persists. Do not jerk the lead.

06How can coming back feel rewarding?Recall+

Recall should be an invitation your dog finds worthwhile. Practise with little distraction and welcome every return. Suitable safety measures and gradual practice help build reliability; repeated shouting or frustration will not do that.

Three small steps

  1. Agree on one recall cue.
  2. Practise nearby and reward coming back.
  3. Use a harness and long line outdoors.

When to take a step back: If they cannot respond, make the task easier. Only allow off-lead time where permitted and safe.

07What helps my dog settle?Finding calm+

Rest needs a suitable space and attention to your dog's needs. Make undisturbed relaxation part of everyday life. Let voluntary quiet moments grow; your dog does not need exhaustion or a rigid position to relax.

Three small steps

  1. Offer a comfortable spot away from activity.
  2. Reward calm behaviour quietly.
  3. Protect sleep and retreat from interruptions.

When to take a step back: Reduce stimulation if they cannot settle. Seek veterinary advice if restlessness persists or begins suddenly.

08How can time alone feel safer?Time alone+

Dogs differ in how easily they cope alone. Start with small separations while you are still at home and watch whether your dog stays comfortable. Arrange company when longer absences are beyond their current ability.

Three small steps

  1. Offer a familiar resting area.
  2. Practise very short separations gently.
  3. Increase only while your dog remains relaxed.

When to take a step back: If they pant, pace or call out, return and shorten practice. Get help for ongoing separation distress; do not leave them to endure it.

When observation isn't enough.

If behaviour changes suddenly, severe restlessness persists or you suspect pain, have your dog examined by a vet. For recurring fear or conflicts, qualified behavioural support using reward-based methods can help.

RSPCA · Support with behavioural changes · Dogs Trust · Possible causes of restlessness

Our approach & sources

We observe needs, offer choices and learn through rewards. Fear, pain and intimidation have no place here. Krishnamurti inspires our outlook; practical exercises draw on the specialist sources linked below.

Applying Krishnamurti’s ideas to life with dogs is our own interpretation. The linked specialist sources inform the practical suggestions; these are not an individual treatment plan.
