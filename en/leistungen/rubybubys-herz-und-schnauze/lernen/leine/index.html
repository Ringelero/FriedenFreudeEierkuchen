Out together

Practise loose-lead walking and still enjoy walks

Reward earlier, break the pull-and-return cycle and separate practice from everyday management.

At home, your dog walks beside you. Outside the front door, they pull towards the next tree. You stop; they return, eat a treat and rush forward again. This guide helps change that cycle: reward earlier, make practice manageable and find a workable approach for the rest of the walk.

First decide what is happening

If your dog pulls towards something interesting, loose-lead practice may fit. If they are trying to escape a sound or dog, they first need distance. Waiting in place for a slack lead can keep them where they feel uncomfortable. Dogs Trust recommends calm alternative routes and sometimes continuing to move rather than stopping. Dogs Trust: managing strong reactions outside.

Your initial goal is a short distance together without tension on the lead. Constant eye contact is not required.

Prepare a manageable stretch

Choose a quiet place with space, comfortable equipment and easily reached food rewards. A fixed-length lead suits practice. Equipment supports learning; an “anti-pull” promise does not replace it. Dogs Trust: preparation and equipment.

One practical arrangement is: “We practise on this quiet stretch; then it is time to explore again.” This does not make a busy main road a suitable training location.

Reward the shared step

Choose a side initially. Start with your dog beside you and a slack lead. Take one step. If the lead stays slack, feed by your leg. Begin with few enough steps to reward before the next surge. Only then increase the distance. Dogs Trust: building short practice sessions.

Food placement matters: feeding far in front or across your path invites moving forward or crossing. Feed on the chosen side, facing the direction of travel. Battersea: reward placement.

If the lead tightens in a safe, quiet place, pause without jerking it. Continue when there is slack again. If it happens immediately every time, shorten the next stretch or change location. Repeated difficulty is no reason to apply more pressure. Dogs Trust: building loose-lead skills.

The rest of the walk needs its own plan

Plan sniffing breaks and signal the end of a practice stretch, for example with “go sniff”. Your dog can use more space while exploring where the surroundings are safe. Battersea: applying practice to walks.

For necessary toilet walks, choose a quieter route and time rather than treating the whole walk as a test. If a stretch is too much, the walk can be shorter, with suitable enrichment at home. This makes everyday life easier while you work on walking skills. Dogs Trust: help with currently difficult walks.

When you need to leave a situation, a previously practised turn cue helps: say “this way”, initially show a gentle turn with a food hand and reward following. Later, use an empty hand and give the reward from your pouch. Start without approaching traffic or dogs. A newly spoken word alone cannot guide your dog in a difficult encounter. Battersea: teaching a direction cue.

When it gets difficult

Your problemWhat to change
Returning, eating and surging forward again.Reward the first shared step before the surge. Do not always wait for pulling and returning.
It works indoors but not outside.Start with a very short stretch again outdoors. Add distraction and distance separately.
Your dog trips you up.Check your food hand: does it draw them across your feet? Feed on the chosen side.
Your dog stares, barks or tries to escape.Increase distance and leave calmly. Better treats alone do not resolve repeated overwhelm.

The first three adjustments follow Battersea's troubleshooting guide; the last follows Dogs Trust's guidance on reactivity. Seek individual support if you cannot handle your dog safely. Sudden changes also need veterinary assessment. Dogs Trust: when support is needed.

A small comparison tells you more than one good day

Our suggested note: “Quiet courtyard, a few shared steps, food by the leg. The open gate made it difficult.” Next time, change one element, such as distance from the gate. This shows which conditions already work. There is no fixed timetable for a “finished” dog.
